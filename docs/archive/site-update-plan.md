# Mechanical Superiority — site update

Draft copy and a work plan for turning the blog into something that also brings in work.
Everything in `[brackets]` needs Mark's input. Copy is written to sound like him, not like a
marketing agency — edit freely, but don't sand off the voice. The voice is the differentiator.

## 1. The problem in one paragraph

The tagline says "Custom engineering solutions, education for all things mechanical." The site
delivers the education half and none of the solutions half. There is no services page, no photos
of finished work, no location, no phone number, and the About page is still the default Ghost text
about subscriptions. A shop owner or contracting officer who lands here learns that Mark
understands engine dynamometers, and nothing about whether he can build them a weldment by
October. Meanwhile a "Coming soon" post from 2022 is still published on the front page.

## 2. Structure to build

Six pages. In Ghost, Services / Work / About / Contact are **Pages** (not posts), and the blog
posts stay as posts.

| Page | Purpose | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Home | Says what the shop does and who it's for, in five seconds | Rebuild |
| Services | The capability list, in plain language | New |
| Work | Photos of finished parts with one line each | New — needs photos |
| Writing | The essays, as proof of expertise | Exists (the blog) |
| About | Who Mark is, background, where the shop is | Rewrite (currently boilerplate) |
| Contact | Phone, email, what to send for a quote | New |

Also: unpublish or delete the "Coming soon" post. It's the second thing on the homepage.

### Services

> ## What this shop does
>
> ### CAD design and drawings
> Part design, assemblies, all the way up to full machine design, plus the drawings to go with
> them. Reverse-engineering is a specialty: bring in a part that broke and has no print, or a

>
> ### Precision machining
> A Bridgeport knee mill handles small precision work — parts, fixtures, modifications, and
> one-off replacements for equipment nobody supports anymore.
>
> ### Welding
> MIG on carbon steel. TIG on aluminum and stainless. Repairs, weldments, welded tube frames,
> and structures built from a drawing or from scratch.
>
> ### CNC plasma cutting
> 2D profiles cut from sheet and plate. Combined with the CAD side, a 3D concept gets flattened
> into individual pieces, nested, and cut as a kit ready to weld — which is often the fastest and
> cheapest way to get from an idea to a welded assembly.
>
> ### Riveted sheet metal
> Design and construction of riveted assemblies. For some applications riveting beats welding
> outright: no heat distortion, no warped panels, and serviceable later. Knowing which method a
> job actually calls for is part of the service.
>
> ### Putting it together
> One shop, all of the above. A concept becomes a model, becomes a cut kit, becomes a welded and
> machined assembly, becomes a finished thing on a truck. No handoffs between four vendors, no
> drawing package thrown over a wall to a shop that never spoke to the designer.
>
> **Typical work:** weldments, frames, brackets, guards, covers, skids, racks and enclosures ·
> trailers, truck bodies and equipment upfits · riveted and welded sheet-metal structures ·
> replacement parts for obsolete equipment · drawing packages for parts that need reproducing.
>
> **Working with other shops:** if you're a shop that's full, missing a capability, or needs a
> second source, call. Overflow work and referrals go both directions.

### Work

Needs photos before it can go live. Six to ten finished pieces, one line of caption each in this
pattern: **what it is — what the problem was — what was done.** For example:

> **Aluminum equipment frame.** Customer had a sketch and a load requirement. Modeled, nested,
> plasma cut, TIG welded, delivered in [X] weeks.

Photos matter more than the words. Phone camera is fine. Shoot in daylight or against a clean
background, get the whole part in frame, and include one shot of something mid-process — sparks,
a part on the mill, a nested plate on the table. Those read as real in a way that catalog shots
don't.

## 4. Ghost mechanics

1. **Pages vs posts.** Create Services, Work, About, and Contact as Pages. Add them to the primary
   navigation in Settings → Navigation, in this order: Services · Work · Writing · About · Contact.
2. **Replace the About page** — the current one is Ghost's stock subscription copy and is doing
   active damage.
3. **Unpublish "Coming soon."**
4. **Site meta description** (Settings → General): *Custom design and fabrication in Fort Collins,
   Colorado. CAD, CNC plasma cutting, welding, machining, and sheet metal — one shop from concept
   to finished part.* This is what shows up in search results.
5. **Upload the capability statement PDF** to Ghost and link it from Contact and Services. Same
   file he hands out in person.
6. **Keep the subscribe box** but move it below the fold on the homepage. It's right for the blog
   and wrong as the first thing a paying customer sees.
7. **Location in the footer.** "Fort Collins, Colorado" should appear on every page — it's how
   local searches find him and how out-of-state buyers work out freight.

## 5. Writing, going forward

The essays are the moat. Nobody hires a fabricator because of a blog, but plenty of people hire
one because they read something and thought *this person actually knows what they're doing.*

- Finish the Power vs. Torque series. Parts 2 and 3 are promised in the text and haven't appeared;
  a promised follow-up that never came is worse than not promising it.
- Shop-adjacent topics carry the same authority and pull in customers rather than car enthusiasts:
  when riveting beats welding, why that part failed at the weld, what a drawing actually needs on
  it before a shop can quote it, how to describe a part you need so you don't get a useless quote.
- Two or three a year is plenty. The essays don't expire.

## 6. The name is settled — now make it consistent

Mechanical Superiority LLC is the business. That resolves the structural question, and it means the
blog's personality is now the brand's personality, which is an advantage almost no fabrication shop
has. A few things follow from it:

- **Use the exact registered name everywhere.** However the name reads on the Colorado filing is
  how it should read on SAM.gov, the capability statement, quotes, and invoices. Federal
  registrations get rejected over mismatches between the legal name, the name on the bank account,
  and the name on the IRS record.
- **Put "LLC" in the site footer** with the city: *Mechanical Superiority LLC · Fort Collins,
  Colorado*. It costs nothing and reads as a real company rather than a hobby blog.
- **Get off Gmail.** A `mark@mechanicalsuperiority.com` address on a domain he already owns is a
  fifteen-minute change and does real work with buyers who are deciding whether to send a
  stranger a drawing package.
- **Register the LLC in SAM.gov, not Mark personally.** The UEI and CAGE attach to the entity, and
  the past performance he builds accrues to the company.
- **The blog is an asset on the federal side, not a liability.** I flagged earlier that the name
  might read as unserious to a contracting officer. With an LLC behind it and a services page in
  front of it, the essay reads as an engineer who knows his subject cold. Leave the jokes in the
  writing; keep the Services, Work and Contact pages straight.
