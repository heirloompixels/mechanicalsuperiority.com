How one part gets drawn

Quick, one-at-a-time jobs are the point, not the problem.

{# Numbered by hand: Mark's 03a is an optional step between 03 and 04. #}

  1. Send it

    Ship me the broken part (or the remaining pieces), pictures of the application, and any partial or complete prints that you might have. Many times you may not even need to send the physical part for me to recreate it; just drawings with dimensions and pictures with a tape measure in-frame.

  2. Measure

    I will measure what I can on my end, and work directly with you to make sure we correctly measure relevant critical dimensions that I can't physically touch. We will communicate to make sure everything is going to fit, and we are all on the same page with what needs to be made.

  3. Model/drawings

    I will create a 3D model with dimensioned shop drawings that any machine shop can use to make your part.

  4. Rapid prototyping (where appropriate)

    One useful extra step might be to have a cheap plastic model 3D-printed so that you can bolt it onto the machine to ensure a proper fit before cutting metal. There are lots of rapid prototyping businesses around, so this part should be easier than you might think to find in your local area. Time-and-location-permitting, I can also print one and bring it to your site.

  5. Cut

    Take your model/drawings to your machine shop of choice, or ask Mechanical Superiority for a quote to make small runs in-house.

Why a small shop wants the one-off

Most established shops want long production runs that keep their people busy for months. A single replacement part is an interruption to them. Here it's the job: variable work, which is the interesting kind.

Oil and gas operations, manufacturing plants, mines and farms all run machines that have outlived the companies that built them. Mark spent years in mining and industrial service, and in agricultural technology, before opening this shop.
